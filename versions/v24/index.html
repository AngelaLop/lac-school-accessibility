The Challenge

Geographic Isolation Drives Educational Inequality

Distance Without Roads

Across Latin America and the Caribbean, millions of rural children live hours from the nearest school. Without paved roads, even short distances become grueling treks through difficult terrain.

15 km = unreachable for families without transport

Mountains and Impassable Terrain

Andean highlands and Central American ranges create physical walls between communities and schools. Children must navigate steep slopes and unpredictable weather just to attend class.

Secondary access drops to 67.6% within 30 min walking

Rivers Without Bridges

In the Amazon basin and Caribbean lowlands, rivers without bridges force families to rely on canoes and seasonal crossings. When waters rise, schools become completely inaccessible.

Rainy season can double or triple travel times
The first step to closing the gap is measuring how far every child must travel to reach a classroom.

Methodology

How It Works

The Fast Marching Method on friction surfaces models real-world travel time from any point to the nearest school.

Locate & Geolocate All Schools

We compile national school registries and satellite imagery to geolocate all 532,650 K-12 schools across 21 countries. Of these, 447,000 are public and 86,000 are private. Our georeferencing accuracy reaches 96%, creating the most comprehensive school location dataset in the region.

Build Friction Surfaces

We construct spatial models of movement difficulty that account for terrain slope, land cover type, road networks, rivers, and other geographic obstacles. Each pixel on the map gets a "cost" value representing how hard it is to cross, whether by foot or motorized transport.

Calculate Travel-Time Isochrones

The Fast Marching Method propagates a wavefront outward from every school location, computing the minimum travel time from any point on the map. The result: continuous travel-time surfaces for both walking and motorized access, enabling isochrone analysis at continental scale.
