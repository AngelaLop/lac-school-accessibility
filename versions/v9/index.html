Mapping School Accessibility in Latin America and the Caribbean: A Fast Marching Method Approach

López, A. (2026)

Inter-American Development Bank, Social Sector Department

Working Paper · March 2026

Abstract

This paper presents a geospatial framework for estimating travel time to the nearest school facility across Latin America and the Caribbean. Leveraging geocoded school census data, WorldPop population grids, and global friction surface rasters, we implement the Fast Marching Method (FMM) to compute continuous cost-distance surfaces at approximately one-kilometer resolution. The methodology captures terrain, land cover, and infrastructure constraints that shape actual travel conditions for school-age children. Pilot results from Panama indicate that 98.1% of primary-age children reside within 30 minutes of a school, while significant accessibility gaps persist in indigenous comarcas and riverine areas. We extend the analysis to 21 countries, documenting a pronounced education-level gradient: pre-primary and secondary facilities are systematically less accessible than primary schools. The resulting dataset constitutes the first harmonized, region-wide measure of physical school accessibility for the LAC region.

Keywords: school accessibility, travel time, Fast Marching Method, Latin America, friction surfaces, geospatial analysis, education infrastructure

1. Introduction

Physical access to schooling remains one of the most fundamental determinants of educational attainment in developing regions. While enrollment ratios across Latin America and the Caribbean have improved substantially over the past two decades, aggregate statistics obscure persistent spatial inequalities. Children in remote, rural, and indigenous communities frequently face travel times measured in hours rather than minutes—a barrier that depresses attendance, increases dropout risk, and compounds existing socioeconomic disadvantage.

Despite the policy relevance of these disparities, no harmonized, region-wide measure of physical school accessibility has existed for LAC. National education management information systems (EMIS) vary in coverage and geocoding quality; household surveys capture self-reported travel times for enrolled students only, missing the most marginalized children entirely. This paper addresses the gap by constructing a geospatial model of travel time to the nearest school facility across 21 countries, spanning pre-primary through upper-secondary education levels.

The contribution is threefold. First, we assemble and harmonize geocoded school census records covering over 400,000 educational facilities. Second, we implement the Fast Marching Method on global friction surfaces to produce continuous travel-time rasters at approximately one-kilometer resolution. Third, we overlay WorldPop population grids to estimate the share of school-age children at each accessibility threshold, producing policy-relevant coverage indicators at subnational, national, and regional scales.

2. Data

The analysis draws on three primary data sources. School census records are obtained from national Ministries of Education via official EMIS platforms or through data-sharing agreements with the Inter-American Development Bank. Each record includes, at minimum, the geographic coordinates of the facility, the education levels offered (pre-primary, primary, lower-secondary, upper-secondary), and the operational status as of the most recent census year.

Population data are sourced from the WorldPop constrained individual countries datasets (2020 vintage), which provide estimated population counts at 100-meter resolution, disaggregated by age group. We extract cohorts corresponding to pre-primary (ages 3–5), primary (ages 6–11), lower-secondary (ages 12–14), and upper-secondary (ages 15–17) populations.

Friction surfaces are taken from the Malaria Atlas Project (MAP) global friction surface (Weiss et al., 2020), which encodes the estimated travel time per meter of ground traversal. The surface integrates road networks, land cover classification, topographic slope, and hydrological barriers at approximately one-kilometer resolution. Water bodies are assigned high but finite friction values to permit modeled canoe or boat crossings where overland alternatives do not exist.

The friction surface methodology captures precisely these differences in terrain and infrastructure quality.

3. Methodology

We compute travel-time surfaces using the Fast Marching Method (FMM), an efficient numerical algorithm for solving the Eikonal equation on discrete grids. The Eikonal equation governs the propagation of a wavefront through a medium of spatially varying speed and takes the form:

|∇T(x)| · F(x) = 1

where T(x) denotes the minimum travel time from source to location x, and F(x) is the local speed (inverse of friction) at x.

FMM solves this equation by propagating a front outward from source locations—in our case, geocoded schools—across all grid cells in order of increasing travel time. The algorithm maintains a narrow band of “trial” cells at the frontier, updates travel-time estimates using an upwind finite-difference scheme, and guarantees that each cell is visited exactly once. Computational complexity is O(N log N) for a grid of N cells, making it tractable for national and regional-scale analyses.

For each education level, we seed the FMM with the set of schools offering that level and compute the minimum travel time from every populated grid cell to the nearest facility. We then overlay age-specific WorldPop population grids to compute coverage rates: the share of the relevant school-age population residing within a given travel-time threshold (e.g., 15, 30, 60, or 120 minutes).

The methodology assumes travel along least-cost paths as determined by the friction surface. It does not model road congestion, public transit schedules, or household vehicle ownership. These limitations are partially offset by the friction surface’s empirical calibration against observed travel-time data from Google and other sources (Weiss et al., 2020).

4. Results

We report initial results from the Panama pilot, which provides the most complete data for validation purposes. Of the approximately 620,000 primary-age children (ages 6–11) identified in the WorldPop grid, an estimated 98.1% reside within 30 minutes of travel time to the nearest primary school. Coverage drops to 94.3% for pre-primary facilities, 91.7% for lower-secondary, and 83.6% for upper-secondary—confirming a steep education-level gradient in physical accessibility.

Spatial disaggregation reveals that the national aggregate masks substantial subnational variation. In Panama City and Colón, primary coverage exceeds 99.5% at the 30-minute threshold. In the Emberá-Wounaan and Guna Yala comarcas, by contrast, coverage falls below 80%, with median travel times exceeding 45 minutes. These areas are characterized by sparse road networks, riverine transport dependence, and dispersed settlement patterns.

Cross-country results confirm that the education gradient is a regional phenomenon rather than a Panama-specific artifact. Across the 21 countries in our sample, the median coverage rate at 30 minutes is 96.8% for primary, 91.2% for lower-secondary, and 79.4% for upper-secondary schools. The gradient is steepest in countries with large rural and indigenous populations, including Bolivia, Guatemala, Honduras, and Peru.

Key finding: At the 60-minute threshold, approximately 12.7 million school-age children across the region reside beyond reasonable walking distance to the nearest secondary school facility, compared to 2.1 million for primary schools.

6. Conclusion

This paper demonstrates that the Fast Marching Method, applied to globally available friction surfaces and geocoded school registries, can produce granular, policy-relevant measures of school accessibility at scale. The resulting travel-time surfaces and coverage indicators offer several advantages over existing approaches: they are spatially continuous rather than administrative-boundary-dependent, they capture terrain and infrastructure constraints ignored by Euclidean distance measures, and they are reproducible across countries using a common methodology.

The education-level gradient documented here—whereby pre-primary and secondary schools are systematically less accessible than primary facilities—carries direct implications for infrastructure investment planning. Ministries seeking to expand coverage at these levels can use the travel-time surfaces to identify optimal locations for new facilities, targeting areas where marginal investments yield the largest reductions in travel time for underserved populations.

Important limitations remain. The friction surface does not capture seasonal variation in road conditions or waterway navigability, which can dramatically alter accessibility in tropical lowland areas. Geocoding quality varies across countries, and incomplete registries may underestimate true coverage. Future work should integrate temporal variation in transport conditions, validate modeled travel times against household-reported data, and extend the analysis to incorporate school quality and capacity constraints alongside physical accessibility.
