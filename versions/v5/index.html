How the Inter-American Development Bank mapped every school in Latin America and the Caribbean—and revealed the invisible geography of educational inequality.

10 minutes

Emma, in Virginia, walks ten minutes to her elementary school. Yurani, in Vaupés, Colombia, walks for more than two hours. Same continent. Same morning. A different world.

Somewhere in the dense rainforest of southeastern Colombia, a seven-year-old girl begins her walk to school before dawn. She follows a dirt trail that threads through the jungle canopy, crosses two streams on foot, and arrives—if the weather holds—just as the first lesson begins. She is one of thousands of children across Latin America and the Caribbean for whom the simple act of reaching a classroom is an odyssey. The Inter-American Development Bank set out to measure that distance, not in kilometres alone, but in time—the most honest unit of access. What they found was a continent divided not just by wealth or politics, but by terrain, by roads that exist on paper but not on the ground, and by the quiet indifference of geography to the aspirations of children.

The LAC School Accessibility Platform is, at its core, a map. But it is a map unlike any that existed before. It plots 532,650 K-12 schools across 21 countries—447,000 public and 86,000 private—with 96 percent of them precisely georeferenced to latitude and longitude coordinates. Against official national registries, the platform achieves 94 percent coverage, making it the most comprehensive inventory of educational infrastructure ever assembled for the region.

The Problem No One Could See

For decades, education policy in Latin America focused on what happened inside classrooms—curricula, teacher training, standardised testing. The question of whether a child could physically reach the classroom in the first place received far less attention. National statistics reported enrolment rates and dropout figures, but they said nothing about the hours lost to impassable roads, swollen rivers, or the sheer remoteness of communities scattered across the Andes, the Amazon basin, and the Caribbean archipelago. Without spatial data, planners were making decisions in the dark. A school might appear adequate on a spreadsheet while being functionally unreachable for the families it was meant to serve.

The scale of the challenge is staggering. Latin America and the Caribbean span roughly 20 million square kilometres, from the Sonoran Desert to Patagonia, from the volcanic highlands of Guatemala to the low-lying coral islands of the Bahamas. The region encompasses mega-cities of 20 million and indigenous communities of 200. Building a single analytical framework to assess school access across this diversity required something that had never been attempted: a unified, georeferenced database of every school, layered over a continent-scale model of how people actually move through the landscape.

The Friction Map

At the heart of the platform lies what researchers call the Friction Map Model, or FMM. The name is apt. Friction, in this context, is everything that slows a child down: mud, mountains, missing bridges, the absence of a paved road. The model begins with satellite imagery of land cover—forest, cropland, urban fabric, water—and overlays it with OpenStreetMap road networks, topographic elevation data, and hydrological maps of rivers and waterways. Each pixel on the resulting surface is assigned a friction coefficient: how long it takes to cross that one-kilometre cell on foot, by vehicle, or by a combination of modes.

From this surface, the algorithm computes least-cost-path travel times: the fastest realistic route from any point in a country to the nearest school. The resolution is one kilometre—fine enough to capture the difference between a paved highway and a jungle footpath, coarse enough to scale across an entire continent. The result is not a single number but a continuous surface, a heat map of access that reveals pockets of isolation invisible to traditional statistics.

Validation came from multiple sources: census microdata, household travel surveys, and field verification in pilot countries. The model was cross-checked against what families actually reported about their commutes, and the correspondence was striking. The friction surface was not a theoretical exercise; it was an empirical portrait of daily life.

“Each pixel is assigned a friction coefficient: how long it takes a child to cross that one kilometre of earth.”

Proof of Concept: Panama

The platform’s first full-scale test came in Panama, a country of 4.3 million people whose geography is a microcosm of the region’s challenges: a modern capital city connected by highways to provincial towns, but separated by dense jungle and mountainous indigenous comarcas from communities where travel is measured in days, not minutes. The pilot analysed more than 3,200 schools and produced a finding that was both reassuring and revealing: 98.1 percent of schools could be reached within 30 minutes by motorised transport. The median walking time to the nearest school was 15 minutes. In urban areas, 99.5 percent of schools were within a 15-minute walk.

But the remaining 1.9 percent told a different story. These were schools in the Ngäbe-Buglé and Guna Yala comarcas, in the Darién Gap, in the highland villages above the continental divide. For the children who depended on them, 30 minutes was an abstraction; their reality was an hour, sometimes two, sometimes an entire morning consumed by the journey. The pilot data gave Panama’s Ministry of Education, for the first time, a precise map of where investment in transport infrastructure, school construction, or alternative delivery models would have the greatest impact. It transformed a vague awareness of inequality into an actionable blueprint. In rural areas, 72 percent of schools were within a 30-minute walk—a figure that, while strong in aggregate, masked deep disparities among individual districts.

A Continent in View

Panama was the proof of concept; the ambition is continental. The platform now spans 21 nations, from Mexico in the north to Uruguay in the south, from Brazil’s 178,400 schools to the Bahamas’ 340. The coverage varies—Chile and Costa Rica report 100 percent georeferencing, while Haiti, still rebuilding after years of crisis, reaches 62 percent—but the trajectory is toward completeness. Countries like Colombia (99%), Brazil (98%), and Mexico (97%) have achieved near-total spatial coverage of their school networks.

The data reveal patterns that transcend national boundaries. Across the region, the deepest access gaps correlate not with poverty alone, but with topography and infrastructure. The Andean highlands, the Amazon basin, the Mosquito Coast of Honduras and Nicaragua, the interior of Guyana—these are the zones where children face the longest journeys. They are also, not coincidentally, the areas with the highest concentrations of indigenous populations and the lowest rates of secondary-school completion. The platform makes this correlation visible, countable, and—for the first time—addressable.

“The deepest access gaps correlate not with poverty alone, but with topography and infrastructure.”

The Twenty-One

The platform encompasses Argentina, Bahamas, Barbados, Belize, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Dominican Republic, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Haiti, Honduras, Jamaica, Mexico, Nicaragua, Panama, Peru, and Uruguay. Together, these nations are home to more than 640 million people and represent nearly every climate, terrain, and level of development found in the Western Hemisphere. The school data set—447,000 public and 86,000 private institutions—captures this diversity at a resolution fine enough to inform decisions at the district level while remaining consistent enough to support regional comparisons.

For policymakers, the implications are immediate. Resources can be directed with precision: not to countries in the aggregate, but to the specific corridors and communities where travel time exceeds acceptable thresholds. For researchers, the platform opens new avenues of inquiry into the relationship between spatial access and educational outcomes. And for the children—for the Yuranis of the continent—it represents a promise that their daily journey has, at last, been measured, and that what has been measured can be changed.
