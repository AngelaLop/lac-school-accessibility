The Challenge

Educational Access Inequality in Latin America

Across Latin America and the Caribbean, millions of children face a daily obstacle that never appears on a syllabus: distance. In remote and rural areas, the nearest school can be hours away by foot, boat, or unpaved road. This geographic barrier drives dropout, limits enrollment, and deepens inequality long before curriculum quality enters the conversation.

Traditional enrollment statistics mask this reality. A school may exist on paper, yet remain effectively unreachable for the communities it is meant to serve. Without granular, geospatial data on travel time, policymakers are planning blind — building schools where access already exists and overlooking the settlements that need them most.

Methodology

How It Works

The platform computes travel time from every populated pixel in the region to its nearest school using the Fast Marching Method — an algorithm that models wave-front propagation across a friction surface.

The friction surface encodes how quickly a person can traverse each cell based on terrain slope, land cover, road networks, waterways, and available transport modes. The algorithm then finds the least-cost path from each origin to the nearest school, yielding a continuous travel-time surface at high resolution.

This approach captures what straight-line distance cannot: a school five kilometers away across a mountain ridge may be far harder to reach than one fifteen kilometers away along a paved highway.

The Education Gradient
